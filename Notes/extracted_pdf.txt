Introduction: The Paradigm Shift in Surgical and
Diagnostic Autonomy
The field of medical robotics is undergoing a profound transition from purely teleoperated
systems—where the robot acts solely as an extension of the human surgeon's hands—toward
supervised autonomy and intelligent physical assistance. Historically, clinical robotics, such as
the widely deployed da Vinci Surgical System, have operated at Level 1 on the surgical
autonomy scale, providing essential tremor filtration and scaled kinematics but relying entirely
on human cognitive and motor control1. The next generation of medical robotics seeks to
achieve Level 2 (task autonomy) and Level 3 (conditional autonomy), enabling systems to
execute complex, multi-step interventions—such as autonomous suturing, tissue retraction,
and intelligent diagnostic ultrasound scanning—while remaining under the supervision of a
human clinician1.
Developing autonomous capabilities for medical applications presents a unique set of
challenges that diverge significantly from traditional industrial or tabletop robotics. Medical
robots operate in highly unstructured, uncertain, and partially observable environments3.
Tissues are deformable, viscoelastic, and prone to unpredictable dynamic changes, such as
bleeding, fluid accumulation, or anatomical shifting during respiration2. Furthermore, sensing
modalities in the operating room are highly specialized, relying on monocular or stereo
endoscopic vision, ultrasound arrays, and complex tool-tissue interaction dynamics rather than
standard RGB-D cameras5.
To overcome these barriers and achieve robust, generalizable performance, the convergence
of Embodied Artificial Intelligence, advanced physics-based simulation, and large-scale
multimodal foundation models is required. This report provides an exhaustive analysis
addressing two critical objectives in the evolution of medical robotics. First, the development
of comprehensive and efficient simulation platforms capable of capturing interaction dynamics,
navigation, and imaging across diverse medical tasks. Second, the evaluation and
benchmarking of advanced learning architectures—specifically Vision-Language-Action (VLA)
models—focusing on sim-to-real transfer, data efficiency, and multimodal learning paradigms5.

Objective 1: Next-Generation Simulation Platform
Development
The foundational layer of any Artificial Intelligence training environment for medical robotics is
the physics and rendering engine. Training robust policies for surgery or diagnostic scanning
requires accurate modeling of soft-tissue deformation, complex tool-tissue contact dynamics,

and the precise reproduction of clinical imaging modalities4.

The Physics of Soft Tissue and Contact Dynamics
Traditional rigid-body physics simulators, such as standard implementations of MuJoCo or
PyBullet, are fundamentally ill-equipped to handle the infinite degrees of freedom and
non-linear behaviors inherent to biological tissue8. To simulate anatomy accurately, modern
simulation frameworks have evolved to rely on distinct mathematical and computational
approaches, balancing biomechanical fidelity with the computational speed necessary for
reinforcement learning.
The Finite Element Method (FEM) has long been the gold standard for biomedical modeling.
FEM discretizes the soft body into thousands of small tetrahedrons to compute stress and
strain matrices using continuum mechanics. Frameworks like the Simulation Open Framework
Architecture (SOFA) utilize hyperelastic material models, such as Neo-Hookean or St.
Venant-Kirchhoff, to accurately simulate large deformations, structural integrity, and tissue
cutting10. However, FEM is exceptionally computationally expensive. Computing the inversion of
large stiffness matrices limits the ability to run the thousands of parallel environments required
for modern Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithms4.
Conversely, engines like NVIDIA PhysX (utilized in Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab) employ
Position-Based Dynamics (PBD). PBD bypasses complex internal force calculations and directly
modifies the positions of simulated particles to satisfy explicit physical constraints, such as
volume preservation and distance thresholds7. While PBD is unconditionally stable and highly
parallelizable on GPUs—allowing for the rapid simulation of cloth, fluids, and basic soft
tissues—it sacrifices physical accuracy compared to FEM, particularly when large position
displacements or high-velocity interactions occur, making topological changes like cutting
mathematically cumbersome4.
To reconcile the trade-off between the physical realism of SOFA and the GPU-accelerated
speed of Isaac Lab, recent advancements have introduced the Material Point Method (MPM)
and Moving Least Squares MPM (MLS-MPM) into surgical simulators. MPM represents tissues
as a collection of continuum Lagrangian particles that exchange mass and momentum with a
background Eulerian grid, naturally handling topological changes like tearing, cutting, and
suturing without the need for complex, real-time tetrahedral re-meshing13. Emerging
frameworks, such as CRESSim, build upon PhysX 5 to provide a high-performance environment
for the da Vinci Research Kit (dVRK), enabling the simulation of contact-rich tasks like blood
suction and tissue grasping by leveraging hybrid rigid-soft coupling4.

High-Fidelity Medical Imaging Simulation
Beyond the physical mechanics of tissue, a medical robotics simulation platform must
accurately render the highly specific sensory feedback utilized by clinicians. Optical rendering
pipelines using standard rasterization or basic ray tracing fail to capture the complex,
noise-heavy realities of stereoscopic endoscopy and ultrasonography.

Robotic Ultrasound: The SonoGym Framework
Ultrasound (US) is an indispensable clinical modality due to its real-time capabilities and
non-invasive nature, making it ideal for orthopedic surgeries, needle insertion guidance, and

anatomical reconstruction3. However, raw US images are characterized by speckle noise,
acoustic shadowing, and reverberation artifacts. The SonoGym simulation platform represents
a state-of-the-art framework specifically designed to train AI agents for robotic ultrasound
procedures3. SonoGym integrates common robotic manipulators, such as the KUKA Med14 and
Franka Panda, interfacing them with highly realistic patient models derived from the
TotalSegmentator dataset, which incorporates 3D CT volumes and anatomical label maps3.

Endoscopic Video Synthesis and Action-Conditioned World Models
For laparoscopic and open surgeries, visual rendering must account for the unique optical
properties of endoscopes, including intense localized lighting, constrained fields of view, and
frequent occlusions from biological fluids6. Instead of relying on manual rendering pipelines,
the field is rapidly shifting toward the use of Video World Models.
The Cosmos-H-Surgical-Simulator is an action-conditioned video diffusion model fine-tuned
on surgical data spanning multiple robotic platforms5. Built upon the Cosmos-Predict 2.5
architecture, this 2-billion-parameter latent video diffusion transformer accepts a single initial
context frame of a surgical scene alongside a sequence of proposed kinematic actions5. It
autoregressively generates future video frames that visually depict the outcome of those
actions. This represents a paradigm shift from traditional FEM physics simulators. Instead of
mathematically calculating tissue deformation, the world model implicitly infers tissue
dynamics, specular lighting changes, and instrument-tissue interactions directly from its latent
understanding of the surgical data distribution5.
Evaluations of the Cosmos-H-Surgical-Simulator utilizing L1 (mean absolute error) and SSIM
metrics demonstrate that the model maintains visual fidelity over 72-frame autoregressive
rollouts. While benchtop scenes maintain low L1 and high SSIM due to controlled lighting and
static backgrounds, tissue-based scenes exhibit higher variance, reflecting the inherent
stochasticity and visual complexity of predicting in-body environments5. Furthermore,
specialized domain-adapted iterations, such as the cosmos3-nanoh-surgical-dvrk checkpoint
hosted on Hugging Face, optimize the nano-scale world model explicitly for dVRK endoscopic
scenes. This model focuses heavily on short-horizon kinematic consistency and near-future
tool motion prediction, utilizing a native 20D dVRK action representation and 16-frame action
chunks to validate simulation behavior in visually constrained environments16.

Objective 2: Learning, Benchmarking, and The Data
Foundation
The most significant barrier to the development of generalized artificial intelligence for medical
robotics has been a structural data bottleneck. Unlike the general robotics community, which
has amassed millions of trajectories in consolidated corpora like the Open X-Embodiment
dataset, medical robotics data collection is severely constrained. Navigating stringent clinical
safety protocols, Institutional Review Board (IRB) approvals, patient privacy regulations, and the
technical complexity of logging high-frequency kinematics from proprietary surgical platforms
has historically limited datasets to single-institution, single-embodiment silos5. Early
benchmarks like JIGSAWS offered merely three hours of data, fundamentally restricting the
generalization capabilities of learned policies5.

Aggregating Global Surgical Data: Open-H-Embodiment
The Open-H-Embodiment dataset systematically resolves this bottleneck, serving as the
critical infrastructure for training surgical foundation models. Representing the largest open
dataset of paired medical robotic video and synchronized kinematics to date, Open-H

comprises 770 hours of data, encompassing over 124,000 trajectories aggregated from 49
global institutions5.

The Evolution of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models
The aggregation of the Open-H dataset has catalyzed the development of large-scale
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models tailored specifically to the medical domain. These
architectures aim to fuse high-level semantic reasoning—derived from vast pre-training on text
and images—with low-level continuous robotic control.

From Autoregressive Decoding to Flow Matching
Early VLA architectures, such as RT-2 and OpenVLA, relied on autoregressive decoding
mechanisms. Visual and linguistic inputs are processed by a Vision-Language Model (VLM),
which predicts continuous action trajectories by discretizing the action space into "action
tokens" and outputting them sequentially6. While highly effective at semantic reasoning,
autoregressive decoding poses severe limitations in contact-rich surgical environments.
Because tokens are generated sequentially, an error in an early token (e.g., an incorrect spatial
coordinate) cannot be revised in subsequent iterations of the same generation step20. In
delicate surgical tasks where millimeter-level precision is required, this compounding error
mechanism frequently leads to catastrophic task failure.
To circumvent this, the field has pivoted toward continuous diffusion and flow-matching
architectures19. Models like
and the GR00T-H series utilize a discrete diffusion or
flow-matching transformer head. Rather than predicting actions token-by-token,
flow-matching architectures generate an entire action chunk (e.g., a trajectory of 50 timesteps)
in parallel. The model begins with pure noise and iteratively refines the continuous-value action
vectors over multiple denoising steps. This iterative refinement allows the model to globally
self-correct the trajectory before execution, leading to substantially smoother, more accurate,
and more resilient surgical maneuvers20. Other emerging approaches, such as the
LingBot-VA-Suture model, decouple visual dynamics modeling from action control entirely.
Operating as a world action model, LingBot-VA utilizes a conditional flow matching mechanism
to predict chunks of future latent visual states while simultaneously decoding the
corresponding end-effector actions via inverse dynamics5.

GR00T-H: The Foundational Surgical VLA
Leveraging the Open-H dataset, researchers developed GR00T-H, a 3-billion-parameter
Vision-Language-Action model specifically optimized for medical robotics5. GR00T-H builds
upon the NVIDIA Isaac GR00T-N1.7 architecture. It utilizes a Cosmos-Reason2-2B backbone to
encode multi-view image observations (such as concurrent endoscopic and wrist cameras)
alongside natural language instructions. The encoded tokens are subsequently processed by a
flow-matching Diffusion Transformer (DiT), which denoises the continuous robotic actions22.
To handle the multi-embodiment nature of the Open-H dataset, GR00T-H implements
embodiment-specific action heads. While the core VLM backbone learns generalized
representations of tissue manipulation, depth estimation, and surgical logic, distinct Multi-Layer
Perceptrons (MLPs) decode the final velocity predictions for specific robotic platforms (e.g.,

dVRK versus CMR Versius)5.
Extensive evaluations on the SutureBot benchmark—a highly structured, long-horizon suturing
task comprising needle pickup, handover, tissue piercing, extraction, and
knot-tying—demonstrated the superiority of the GR00T-H architecture. Baseline
autoregressive models and pure imitation learning Action Chunking Transformers (ACT) failed
entirely, completing 0% of the end-to-end trials. In contrast, GR00T-H achieved a 25%
end-to-end success rate5. The model's robustness was further validated in an ex vivo clinical
setting utilizing skin-on pork belly. Over a 29-subtask sequence requiring needle throwing,
wound opening, and suture cutting, GR00T-H achieved an overall average sub-task success
rate of 64%5. The model exhibited near-perfect execution on structured manipulation
primitives but demonstrated lower efficacy on steps requiring fine-contact tissue coordination,
such as cutting, illuminating the specific boundary where current visual-kinematic models
struggle to replicate tactile feedback.
Crucially, GR00T-H exhibited remarkable data efficiency. When fine-tuned on only 33% of a
target dataset, GR00T-H matched the performance of specialized ACT models trained on the
full dataset, proving that prior exposure to the massive Open-H corpus instills strong,
transferrable surgical priors5.

Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap and Hierarchical
Control
Deploying foundation models directly to physical robots in the operating room presents
immediate safety and precision challenges. To bridge the gap between high-level surgical
planning and low-level physical control, hierarchical frameworks and specialized kinematic
representations are essential.

Overcoming Hardware Hysteresis with Hybrid-Relative Actions
A persistent challenge in bridging the sim-to-real gap, or transferring policies between
different surgical robots, is hardware inaccuracy. Cable-driven robotic systems, such as the
widely used da Vinci Research Kit (dVRK), suffer from severe mechanical hysteresis, cable
stretch, and potentiometer inaccuracies, particularly in their passive setup joints25. A policy
trained to output absolute Cartesian coordinates (camera-centric actions) will fail
catastrophically when deployed on the physical robot, as the robot's internal proprioceptive
estimation of its end-effector position frequently diverges by several centimeters from physical
reality25.
To resolve this, advanced imitation learning frameworks have adopted Hybrid-Relative Action
representations. By predicting differential spatial translations relative to the static endoscope
camera tip, while simultaneously predicting delta rotations relative to the moving end-effector
frame, the policy mathematically bypasses the inaccurate joints of the robotic arm25. As
demonstrated in the Surgical Robot Transformer (SRT) evaluations across tissue lifting, needle
handover, and knot-tying tasks, policies trained on hybrid-relative actions can seamlessly adapt
to physical disruptions—such as a researcher manually altering the setup joints
mid-operation—while absolute-action policies immediately crash25. This representation forms

the fundamental requirement for scaling models across datasets that lack pristine, calibrated
kinematics.

The SRT-H Hierarchical Architecture
The Hierarchical Surgical Robot Transformer (SRT-H) framework exemplifies the integration of
language models for real-time error correction. Operating on the complex task of clipping and
cutting the cystic duct and artery during an ex vivo cholecystectomy, the SRT-H framework
separates the control problem into two interacting policies:
1.​ High-Level (HL) Policy: Utilizing a Swin Transformer vision encoder paired with a
Transformer Decoder, the HL policy analyzes a temporal history of endoscopic frames
and outputs natural language commands. Crucially, the HL policy acts as a real-time
supervisor. If it detects a deviation (e.g., the instrument grasps the wrong tissue or
overshoots the duct), it generates corrective language instructions (e.g., "move left arm
towards me") rather than nominal task instructions2.
2.​ Low-Level (LL) Policy: An EfficientNet-based network processes the real-time visual
feed and conditions its output on the language embeddings generated by the HL policy
(via Feature-wise Linear Modulation, or FiLM, layers). The LL policy directly decodes the
20-dimensional kinematic vector necessary to drive the robot arms2.
This hierarchical structure allows the system to self-correct during long-horizon tasks. In
extensive ex vivo trials, SRT-H demonstrated a 100% success rate across eight unseen porcine
gallbladders, successfully executing 17 distinct subtasks and autonomously generating an
average of six self-corrections per procedure. Ablation studies confirmed that the removal of
the high-level language correction module resulted in a severe degradation of recovery
capabilities, emphasizing the necessity of semantic oversight in unpredictable clinical
environments2.

Benchmarking Frameworks and MDP Formulations
As foundation models scale, establishing rigorous, standardized benchmarks becomes
imperative to quantify progress and identify architectural bottlenecks. In the medical domain,
generic robotic benchmarks are insufficient to test the nuances of clinical intervention.

SurgVLA-Bench
The SurgVLA-Bench represents the first comprehensive evaluation framework for
Vision-Language-Action models in laparoscopic environments6. Built upon the SurRoL
simulator, the benchmark establishes a clinically relevant, hierarchical taxonomy of surgical
tasks:
●​ Level 1 (Atomic Tasks): Basic targeting and acquisition, such as picking up a surgical
needle or applying electrocoagulation to a specific, unobstructed blood spot on an
organ6.
●​ Level 2 (Conditional Tasks): Introduces visual distractors and dynamic
decision-making. For example, the policy must identify and clip a specific bleeding
vessel segment while actively ignoring adjacent, non-bleeding vasculature6.
●​ Level 3 (Composite Tasks): Requires multi-step spatial reasoning and complex tool
coordination, such as a complete hemostasis procedure involving grasping gauze,

transporting it across the anatomical cavity, and applying sustained pressure to a target
hemorrhage site6.
Evaluations on SurgVLA-Bench reveal that while current VLAs possess strong semantic
localization capabilities (easily identifying anatomical targets), they consistently fail at the point
of physical contact due to the constrained endoscopic field of view, frequent instrument
occlusions, and the ultra-high precision required for tool-tissue interaction6.

Evaluating Reinforcement Learning in Diagnostic Robotics
In diagnostic and image-guided applications, specifically robotic ultrasound, evaluation metrics
and Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulations differ significantly from surgical manipulation.
The SonoGym framework evaluates agents across three specific mathematical formulations to
capture the unique constraints of ultrasound tasks3:
1.​ US Navigation (POMDP): The objective is to move the probe along the skin to locate an
unknown target anatomy using solely the real-time US image as feedback. This is
formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process. Deep RL algorithms like
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) demonstrate high stability and superior rotation
tracking compared to Imitation Learning baselines, which often suffer from higher
positional variance3.
2.​ Bone Surface Reconstruction (Submodular MDP): This task requires optimizing the
sweep path of the probe to generate a dense 3D reconstruction of a vertebra. Because
the reward relies on the history of unique spatial points already scanned (a non-additive
trajectory reward), the problem is mathematically formulated as a submodular MDP.
Submodular PPO agents significantly outperform heuristic, open-loop scanning paths,
generating efficient, circular sweep patterns that maximize volumetric coverage while
simultaneously minimizing the total trajectory length3.
3.​ US-Guided Surgery (State-Wise Constrained MDP): Involving the safe driving of a
surgical drill into a vertebra while adhering to strict anatomical boundaries, this task
demands rigorous safety constraints. By incorporating Safe Reinforcement Learning
architectures (e.g., SafeRPlan utilizing safety filters), policies ensure high "safe ratio"
compliance. The state-wise constrained MDP maintains conservative margins around
critical tissues, heavily penalizing any trajectory deviation that threatens to breach
defined anatomical safety corridors, a capability that standard Action Chunking
Transformers often fail to replicate reliably3.

Future Research Directions
The rapid convergence of high-fidelity simulation, massive open-source data aggregation, and
flow-matching foundation models opens several critical pathways for future research in
medical robotics.
1. Unified World Models for Predictive Control: Current world models, such as the
Cosmos-H-Surgical-Simulator and cosmos3-nanoh-surgical-dvrk, are primarily utilized for
offline video generation, synthetic data generation, and automated policy validation5. The next
technological frontier involves deploying these models online as predictive engines within a
Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework. By autoregressively predicting the visual and

physical outcomes of thousands of potential action sequences in real-time, the robotic system
can dynamically select trajectories that avoid tissue damage or excessive suture tension,
effectively functioning as an intuitive "clinical imagination" engine5. Developing domain-specific
metrics to evaluate the spatial consistency of generated instruments against ground-truth tool
trajectories remains an open challenge5.
2. Integrating Haptic and Tactile Modalities: While the Open-H dataset provides
unprecedented visual and kinematic diversity, true surgical mastery requires tactile feedback.
The integration of force-torque sensors and vision-based tactile sensors (e.g., GelSight) into
the VLA tokenization pipeline is essential19. Future neural architectures must utilize
cross-attention mechanisms to fuse visual occlusion events with sudden spikes in tactile
impedance. This multimodal fusion will govern tasks requiring delicate force modulation, such
as tissue dissection, suturing tensioning, and the detection of pathological anomalies beneath
the tissue surface19.
3. Continual Learning and Surgeon-in-the-Loop Interventions: Medical environments are
non-stationary; anatomical presentations vary wildly between patient demographics, and
surgical tools degrade mechanically over time. Systems must move beyond static post-training
phases. Implementing continual learning algorithms based on real-time DAgger loops—where
a supervising surgeon can verbally or manually correct a deviating policy (as demonstrated in
the SRT-H framework)—will allow systems to dynamically update their latent representations
without undergoing computationally exhaustive complete retraining cycles2.
4. Advancing Differentiable Simulation: To fully close the sim-to-real gap, simulators must
evolve beyond discrete physics engines. The integration of fully differentiable frameworks (e.g.,
Taichi, Brax), operating alongside Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), will allow
simulation parameters—such as tissue elasticity, Poisson's ratio, and friction coefficients—to be
automatically optimized using gradient descent on real-world surgical footage8. This
continuous alignment between the virtual and physical environments will dramatically
accelerate the generation of zero-shot transferrable policies, ensuring that simulation
platforms remain perfectly synchronized with the physical reality of the operating room8.

Conclusion
The pursuit of autonomy in medical robotics has fundamentally shifted from the design of
narrow, task-specific, hand-crafted control algorithms to the deployment of generalized,
data-driven foundation models. This transition is underpinned by massive collaborative data
initiatives, most notably Open-H-Embodiment, which provide the critical mass of multimodal,
cross-embodiment data required to train Vision-Language-Action policies like GR00T-H.
Simultaneously, advanced simulation frameworks like SonoGym and Cosmos-H, leveraging
hybrid physics engines, deep generative rendering, and latent world models, have
democratized the ability to safely train and benchmark these policies in highly realistic clinical
scenarios.
As architectural paradigms evolve from autoregressive token generation to continuous flow
matching, and as hierarchical control structures increasingly allow for semantic error
correction, the robustness of automated surgical manipulation continues to rise. While fully

autonomous, unmonitored surgery remains a distant prospect, the integration of these
technologies establishes the groundwork for sophisticated, AI-driven surgical copilots capable
of standardizing clinical outcomes, alleviating provider fatigue, and democratizing access to
precision healthcare globally.
